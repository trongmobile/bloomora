When you watch a Bloomora time-lapse you will see nine stages: seed, germination, seedling, vegetative growth, bud formation, bud development, blooming, full bloom, and wilting and seed. This article explains where they come from and what each step means.

## This split is ours

None of the sources we read lists a scheme that matches these nine steps exactly. So treat it as an editorial presentation, not a scientific standard.

Two existing frameworks show why. The BBCH scale, used in agriculture, has ten principal stages numbered 0 to 9, from germination to senescence or dormancy, but it was designed for crops rather than ornamental flowers. New Zealand’s Science Learning Hub uses six stages: germination, establishment, flowering, pollination, fertilisation, and seed and fruit formation. We split the run-up to flowering into smaller steps because that is the part viewers most want to watch.

## What the nine stages describe

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Seed | The embryo sleeps in the seed or bulb, waiting for water, suitable temperature and sometimes a cold season. |
| Germination | The root emerges first, followed by the shoot. |
| Seedling | The first true leaves open and start photosynthesising. |
| Vegetative growth | Stems and leaves grow and the plant stores up energy. |
| Bud formation | The plant switches from making leaves to making flowers; small buds appear. |
| Bud development | The bud swells and petal colour shows through the sepals. |
| Blooming | The sepals part and the outer petals unfold. |
| Full bloom | The flower is fully open, with stamens and pistil exposed for pollination. |
| Wilting and seed | Petals fade and the ovary develops into fruit holding the seeds. |

## How a flower is built

A complete flower has four types of organ on a receptacle. From the outside in they are sepals, petals, stamens (male) and carpels (female). According to Britannica, sepals chiefly guard the unopened bud, and together they form the calyx. Stamens produce pollen; each carpel has a stigma, style and ovary (Wikipedia).

Sepals often outlast the petals and stamens. In the rose they persist until the fruit matures (Britannica) — something you can see in the final stage of the rose simulation, when the hip forms.

## What happens to the petals?

After fertilisation the style, stigma, stamens, petals and sepals typically “wither and die”, while the ovary becomes the fruit (Wikipedia). In kōwhai the petals fall off and the ovary lengthens (Science Learning Hub). Each group of plants does it its own way: studies of orchids show several different post-pollination changes, so do not read this as a rule for every species.

It also connects to deadheading. According to the RHS, removing spent flowers encourages more blooms by redirecting energy away from seed; but if you want fruit or seed, leave the spent flowers on the plant.

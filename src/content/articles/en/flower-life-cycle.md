A flower is only one chapter in a longer story: the story of a plant going from seed to seed. Knowing the main steps of that story helps you read what happens in each Bloomora time-lapse.

## Inside a seed

According to Penn State Extension, a seed contains living embryonic tissue, stored food reserves and a protective seed coat around both. The embryo is a tiny plant; the reserves feed it until it can photosynthesise for itself. A seed does not need sunshine to stay alive — it needs the right conditions to wake up.

## What germination needs

Seeds need the right moisture and oxygen. Because seeds also respire, they can suffocate in overwatered or compacted soil. Each kind of seed has its own minimum, optimum and maximum temperature; the RHS says most seeds germinate best at around 18 °C, with exceptions noted on the packet.

Light depends on the plant. Most seeds germinate best in darkness, but some, such as begonia, primula and coleus, need light to germinate.

## Dormancy and the “fake winter”

Some seeds will not germinate even when conditions look perfect. This is dormancy, which holds seeds back until conditions favour the seedling. Some seeds need a hard coat softened; others need warm and cold periods, known as stratification. You have met the same idea in bulbs: tulip bulbs need roughly 12–16 weeks of cold to flower properly (Iowa State Extension).

## Root first, then shoot

In dicots the root (radicle) emerges first, followed by the shoot. The first seed leaves are often just storage organs; the true leaves that appear later are where photosynthesis really happens. That is why the “germination” stage of a time-lapse looks like little more than a small shoot and a few roots.

## From growing to flowering

For a long time the plant only makes stems and leaves. Flowering begins when the shoot tip stops making vegetative structures and starts making flowers; before that, the plant must also pass a juvenile phase (Wikipedia, Flower induction). This is why a young plant that is grown perfectly may still not flower right away.

## Flower, seed and fruit

Pollination is pollen moving from the anther to the stigma; fertilisation is the fusion of male and female gametes. According to the RHS, pollination triggers hormones that start fruit development; the seed then builds up food reserves, loses water and hardens its coat. The fertilised ovule becomes the seed and the ovary becomes the fruit (Science Learning Hub). That is why the last stage in the simulation is “wilting and seed”, not “death”.

## Annuals, biennials and perennials

According to the RHS, annuals grow, flower, set seed and die within one year. Biennials grow in year one, then flower, set seed and die in year two. Perennials live for multiple years; herbaceous ones usually die back in late autumn and re-sprout each spring.

On Bloomora, sunflower and marigold are examples of annuals; tulip is a perennial bulb; rose and cherry blossom are woody plants that take years to mature. These different rhythms are why the “start to bloom” times of the flowers on the site range from a few weeks to many years.

Most mistakes in growing flowers come from a few basics: the wrong amount of sun, watering the wrong way, soil that does not drain, or planting out too early. This article sums up what extension sources agree on, and points out where they differ.

## How much sun is “full sun”?

Definitions vary between extension services: some count full sun as 6 or more hours of direct sun, and they draw the lines for partial sun and shade at different hour counts. Penn State Extension says most vegetables and flowers need at least 6–8 hours of full sun. Treat these figures as approximate, and read the label for each plant.

## Watering

The RHS advises watering less often but more thoroughly, ideally in the early morning. Evening is acceptable, but leaves left wet overnight invite pests and fungal disease. Water the base of the plant rather than the foliage, and avoid fixed schedules. Established perennials rarely need watering outside extreme drought.

University of Missouri Extension stresses that light daily watering creates shallow root systems, while long, infrequent watering wets the soil about 6–8 in (15–20 cm) deep.

Overwatering does harm too. Poorly drained soil starves roots of oxygen and invites root rot; in containers, excess moisture can kill roots. Signs include yellow patches, brown spots or wilted flowers even though the soil is wet.

## Soil: drainage and pH

According to the RHS, clay drains slowly — wet and cold in winter, baked hard in summer — and organic matter improves it. Sandy soil drains fast and needs plenty of watering. On wet sites, choose plants adapted to wet conditions unless you install drainage.

On acidity: pH 7 is neutral, below 7 acid, above 7 alkaline. The RHS says about 6.5 is a good general-purpose pH, and nutrients are most available at 6.1–7.0. Above 7, iron and manganese become less available.

## Mulch

The RHS recommends at least 5 cm (ideally 7.5 cm) of organic mulch, leaving a 10 cm gap around woody stems. Mulch protects roots from heat and cold, improves drainage in clay and moisture retention in sand. Delay if the soil is waterlogged or frozen.

## Deadheading

Removing spent flowers encourages more blooms by redirecting energy away from seed (RHS). There are two exceptions: if you want fruit or seed, such as rose hips or seed for birds, leave them; and with bulbs, leave the green stalk so the bulb can build up energy.

## Hardening off

Seedlings raised indoors need to get used to the outdoors gradually. The RHS suggests about 2–3 weeks of gradual exposure to cooler temperatures, lower humidity and more air movement; the University of Nebraska–Lincoln suggests 7–10 days, starting with 2–3 hours in shade. These differ, so choose according to the plant. Hardening off does not make frost-tender plants hardy, and skipping it risks cold damage, windburn and sunburn.

## Reading the RHS hardiness scale

The RHS uses ratings from H1a to H7 based on minimum winter temperature, as a guideline only: H1c is 5 to 10 °C; H2 is 1 to 5 °C (will not survive freezing); H3 is −5 to 1 °C; H4 −10 to −5 °C; H5 −15 to −10 °C; H6 −20 to −15 °C; H7 below −20 °C.

On Bloomora, the flower pages cite morning glory as H1c, marigold as H2 and cherry blossom as H6. That means in places with frost the first two should go out after the frosts have passed, while cherry blossom tolerates a cold winter. Plant warmth-loving plants out when nights are reliably above about 7 °C.

Flowers do not open simply because “it is time”. Plants take cues from the environment — night length, a cold season, temperature — and from themselves, such as age, health and stress. This article walks through the main cues, with examples from the flowers on Bloomora.

## Night length: photoperiodism

According to Oregon State Extension, day length controls flowering in most plants, and what matters most is uninterrupted darkness rather than the hours of daylight. Plants fall into three groups.

Short-day plants flower when days are shorter than roughly 12 hours; examples are chrysanthemum, poinsettia and Christmas cactus. Long-day plants need more than about 12 hours of daylight; examples are asters, coneflowers, California poppies, lettuce and spinach. Day-neutral plants flower regardless of day length; examples are tomatoes, corn and cucumbers. Wikipedia lists roses as day-neutral.

The “12 hours” figure is only a rough guide: each plant has its own critical night length (Wikipedia). For chrysanthemum, a greenhouse study of one cultivar found plants flowered under 11-hour days but not under 15-hour days, and its authors say the critical night length is still not well defined (Frontiers in Plant Science).

## Light in the night

Because short-day plants “count” darkness, artificial light at night can upset them. Wikipedia notes that a short-day plant may not flower if light is switched on for a few minutes in the middle of the night. Iowa State Extension likewise warns that street lights can delay or prevent flowering in chrysanthemums. Keeping mums away from night-time light is therefore a practical care tip.

## A cold season: vernalisation

Some plants flower only after a prolonged cold period. This is called vernalisation; typical temperatures are about 1–7 °C, and Wikipedia gives no single duration. Familiar examples are winter wheat, biennials and fruit trees.

The tulip is the clearest illustration. Iowa State Extension says tulip bulbs need about 12–16 weeks at 4–7 °C (40–45 °F) to bloom. That is why in warm climates bulbs are usually pre-chilled before planting.

## Temperature and cherry blossom

For cherry blossom we found no source that calls it vernalisation in the strict sense. What is better established is that spring temperature sets the date: University of Illinois Extension says accumulated heat predicts cherry bloom better than the calendar does, and warming brings earlier bloom — Washington DC cherries bloom nearly a week earlier on average.

## Age and maturity

A plant must be mature enough to flower. Wikipedia describes a juvenile phase that has to end first. The RHS notes that shrubs can sometimes take several years to establish before flowering freely. We found no specific number of years for named woody species in the sources we read, so the milestones on the cherry blossom and rose pages are only wide, low-confidence ranges.

## Stress and pruning

The RHS lists reasons shrubs fail to flower: drought (including the previous year’s), too much nitrogen favouring leaves over flowers, hard pruning that stimulates leaves, and a wet season giving lush growth but few flowers. Rhododendrons and camellias set their buds in late summer, so drought then cuts flowering.

For chrysanthemums, Iowa State advises pinching two to three times from spring to midsummer and stopping by early July for compact plants with more flowers; pinching later can delay flowering.

## In short

Blooming is the result of several signals at once: night length for mums, a cold season for tulips, spring temperature for cherries and age for woody plants. When a Bloomora simulation shows a plant making buds “just in time”, remember that in the real world it depends on the weather and on where you grow it.
